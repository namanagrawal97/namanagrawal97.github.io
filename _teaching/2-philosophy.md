/Through my teaching I aim to inculcate **Curiosity** and **self-reflection** — asking a further question, going back to a source, revising a belief  I inculcate curiosity and self-reflection through three mechanisms: **hands-on learning, revision-based assessment, and a safe, inclusive classroom**. 
### Hands-On Learning
Students learn more from what they do than from what they watch (Freeman et al., 2014). In UDL (Universal Design for Learning) terms, the dissection added a tactile and visual representation of the material in addition to the text. I have been an improv player and leader for the past 7 years, which has made me adept at encouraging students to shed their inhibitions and participate in classroom activities like these. Additionally, I am a member of the Faculty for Undergraduate Neuroscience (FUN) which maintains active resource library of activities for teaching neuroscience concepts.   
### Revision-Based Assessment
Students distribute effort toward whatever is graded (Gibbs & Simpson, 2004); if the graded object is the finished product, students optimize for safety over curiosity. I counter this by making revision an equally weighted part of the grade: each essay runs through a first submission (graded for completeness, not quality), peer and instructor feedback, a revision, and a written reflection on what the student changed and why. I also ensure that the feedback is provided as questions rather than suggestions. Importantly, students may reject any piece of feedback provided given can argue why. This structure converts revision from compliance to ownership and growth.
### A Safe, Inclusive Classroom
The process of peer feedback is bolstered by creating a classroom community that makes students trust each other and allows them to make mistakes. I leverage my seven years of experience as an improv player to integrate community building in the course content. I open class sessions with short, structured community-building exercises — identity circles, diversity BINGO, along with think-pair-share and argue-the-opposite-side. These exercises serve UDL's engagement principle: students are more willing to take intellectual risks once they know the people in the room. I build alternative participation routes for students who have social anxiety or cannot participate due to medical emergencies. 
### Evidence-Based Revision of My Own Teaching
I run the first-submission/feedback/revision cycle on my own teaching. I collect my own formative assessment at different time-scales, daily entry and exit tickets, four-weeks check-in, mid-semester feedback and a qualitative end of semester survey. These surveys are administered in addition to the solitary end-of-term survey administered by the university. I use the feedback I receive to make changes to the course on a fine scale. The daily exit tickets contain “one muddiest point” question, which allows me to explain an unclear concept in the next class itself.
